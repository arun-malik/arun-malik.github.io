The real patch window starts at disclosure, and it is now 14 days

Arun Malik·November 30, 2026·Data story
Data StorySecurity

Join CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog to CVE publication dates and the federal clock looks very different. Half of new entries are now due within 14 days of the CVE going public, a third of deadlines land on weekends, and most looked low-risk to prediction models the day before.

Every KEV entry carries a dateAdded and a dueDate. The chart above is the simple view: in 2025, 226 of 245 new entries (92.2%) gave federal agencies 21 days. Since June 10, 2026, when CISA issued BOD 26-04, 151 of 191 (79.1%) give 3 days or less. Volume is up too: 324 additions through November 27, 45% more than the same period in 2025.

That is the headline everyone will quote. It is also the least useful part of the data, because it starts the clock at the wrong moment and hides when the work actually lands. I joined the catalog to two more public datasets to see what the simple chart does not show.

What the raw data does not show at first glance

The chart above is the proof. The findings below are derived: they come from joining, re-cutting or reasoning across the data, and each one shows how it was derived.

Insight 1

The window that matters is disclosure to deadline, and it shrank from 47 days to 14

KEV measures time from listing to deadline. But your exposure starts when the CVE is published. Joining each entry to its CVE publication date shows the full clock. In 2025 the median entry was due 47 days after the CVE went public. Since June 10, 2026 it is 14 days. Half of new entries (51.3%) are now due within two weeks of disclosure, up from 6.5% in 2025, and 30.7% are due within a single week. CISA is also listing faster: median disclosure to listing fell from 26 days to 7.

So whatWrite your SLA from CVE publication, not from KEV listing: half of new entries are now due within two weeks of disclosure.

How I derived this: For each of the 569 entries added from January 1, 2025, I fetched the CVE record from the CVE Program's cvelistV5 repository and took cveMetadata.datePublished (found for 567). Disclosure to deadline is dueDate - datePublished in days. The chart is a cumulative distribution: for each day on the x axis, the share of entries whose deadline had passed by then.

Insight 2

One in three federal deadlines now lands on a weekend

In 2025 not a single KEV deadline fell on a Saturday or Sunday: nearly every due date was 21 days after a weekday listing, so it landed on a weekday. Since June 10, 60 of 191 deadlines (31%) fall on a weekend, and Friday is now the busiest due day (47). The reason is arithmetic: additions cluster on Tuesdays and Wednesdays, around Patch Tuesday, and three days later is Friday or Saturday. Of the 151 entries with 3 days or less, 52 (34%) contain only one business day. On-call, change freezes and weekend approvals are now part of patch SLAs.

So whatWeekend change approval is now part of patch readiness, not an exception process.

How I derived this: Weekday of each dueDate, for entries added in 2025 and from June 10 to November 27, 2026. Business days count Monday to Friday after dateAdded up to and including dueDate; US federal holidays are not removed, so the real count can be lower.

Insight 3

Prediction scores mostly missed these vulnerabilities before CISA listed them

Many teams prioritise with FIRST's Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS). The day before CISA listed them, 68% of entries added since June 10 had no EPSS score at all or a score under 1%. 52 of the 54 unscored entries had been public for a day or less, so the model had not caught up. And the share that already scored 10% or more fell, from 21.6% in 2025 to 12.6%, because listings now arrive earlier in a CVE's life. If an EPSS threshold is the trigger for your fastest SLA, most of these would not have tripped it in time.

So whatTreat a KEV listing as a trigger on its own. Do not wait for a prediction score to catch up.

How I derived this: For each entry I queried the FIRST EPSS API with date set to the day before dateAdded and bucketed the score. "No EPSS score yet" means the API returned no record for that CVE on that date.

What this means if you are not a federal agency

The patch clock no longer starts at the KEV listing, and it no longer stops for the weekend.

KEV due dates quietly became the default SLA in many enterprise vulnerability programs. Taken together, the three findings change how that SLA should be written: measure from disclosure, not from listing; staff for weekends; and treat a KEV listing as a trigger on its own, not something that waits for a prediction score to cross a threshold.

Three questions for your team: How many hours after a KEV listing do we know whether it touches our estate? Who approves a production change at 6pm on a Friday? And which of our SLAs still depend on a score that may not exist yet?

How these numbers were produced

Base data: the CISA KEV JSON feed, catalog version 2026.11.27 (1,808 entries). I use entries added from January 1, 2025 to November 27, 2026 (569). Remediation window is dueDate - dateAdded; every value in that period is exactly 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 14 or 21 days. Two joins: CVE publication dates from the CVE Program's cvelistV5 repository (datePublished, 567 of 569 found) and FIRST EPSS scores for the day before each listing (API date parameter). Cohorts are 2025, January 1 to June 9 2026, and June 10 to November 27 2026. Each insight states its own derivation. Every chart has its data table and a CSV on this page.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Due date is a proxy for urgency. The KEV dueDate is the deadline for US federal civilian agencies under CISA directives. It is not a legal requirement for private companies and does not measure how fast anyone actually patched.
  • Correlation, not attribution. Short deadlines appeared before BOD 26-04 (the first 3-day due date of 2026 was on January 27). I mark June 10 as context, not as the single cause of the change.
  • Directive details are context only. I describe BOD 26-04 from CISA's directive page and public reporting. No number on this page is computed from the directive; all numbers come from the catalog feed.
  • Grouping the shortest deadlines. I combine 1, 2 and 3 day due dates into one "3 days or less" band, and 5 and 7 day due dates into "5 to 7 days", to keep the chart readable. The CSV keeps the same bands.
  • 2026 is a partial year. November 2026 covers November 1 to 27 only. Year-over-year growth (+45%) compares equal year-to-date windows, not full years.
  • 2021 and 2022 are left out of trend claims. The catalog launched in November 2021 with a large backfill of older CVEs, so early annual totals are not comparable.
  • The catalog changes over time. CISA can edit or remove entries. Numbers reflect the catalog as published on November 27, 2026.
  • The enterprise SLA point is my judgment. The claim that many enterprise programs reuse KEV due dates is an observation from practice, not a measured statistic.
  • Publication date stands in for disclosure. I use the CVE record's datePublished. Some vulnerabilities are discussed or exploited before a CVE is published, so true exposure can start earlier than shown.
  • Two entries lack a publication date. 2 of 191 post-June 10 entries had no CVE record I could fetch; they are excluded from the disclosure analysis only.
  • EPSS is as served, not as rebuilt. Scores are what the FIRST API returns today for each past date. FIRST has updated the EPSS model over time, so scores are not perfectly comparable across periods.
  • Weekends, not holidays. The weekend and business-day counts ignore US federal holidays, so they understate how many deadlines fall on non-working days.
  • Cohorts are not equal length. The post-June 10 cohort covers about five and a half months; shares, not counts, are compared across periods.
