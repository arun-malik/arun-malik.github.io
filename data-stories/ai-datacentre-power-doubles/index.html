Data centres could take up to half of new US electricity demand by 2028

Arun Malik·December 7, 2026·Data story
Data StoryInfrastructure

The IEA says global data centre electricity will more than double by 2030. Join that to the US Department of Energy's LBNL report and two less obvious things appear: data centres could absorb between a sixth and a half of all new US demand, and the gap between the forecasts is now bigger than the entire industry was in 2023.

Every AI roadmap I review has a GPU line. Far fewer have a megawatt line. The chart above is the simple version, from the International Energy Agency's Energy and AI report: data centres used about 415 TWh in 2024, around 1.5% of global electricity, and the IEA's Base Case reaches about 945 TWh by 2030. By 2035 its cases range from about 700 to 1,700 TWh.

"More than double" is easy to say and hard to plan against, because 1.5% of global power sounds small. The constraint is local, and the US is where most of the demand sits (about 45% in 2024). So I joined the IEA numbers to the US Department of Energy's 2024 report on US data centre energy use, produced by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL).

What the raw data does not show at first glance

The chart above is the proof. The findings below are derived: they come from joining, re-cutting or reasoning across the data, and each one shows how it was derived.

Insight 1

Data centres could take between a sixth and a half of all new US electricity demand

LBNL reports data centres used 176 TWh in 2023, 4.4% of US electricity, and projects 325 to 580 TWh by 2028, 6.7% to 12%. Those percentages also pin down LBNL's implied US total: about 4,000 TWh in 2023 and about 4,850 TWh in 2028. So the whole country adds roughly 850 TWh of demand, and data centres account for 149 TWh (17.5%) in the low case and 404 TWh (48.5%) in the high case. A sector using under 5% of US power could drive up to half of its growth, competing with factory reshoring and electrification for the same interconnection queues.

So whatA sector using under 5% of US power could drive up to half of its growth. Power is a local, competitive constraint, not a rounding error.

How I derived this: Implied US total = data centre TWh / data centre share: 176 / 4.4% = 4,000 TWh (2023); 325 / 6.7% = 4,851 TWh and 580 / 12% = 4,833 TWh (2028). Data centre share of growth = (2028 data centre TWh minus 176) / (2028 total minus 4,000). LBNL does not publish this ratio; it is my derivation from its rounded figures.

Insight 2

The low case is just the last decade continuing, and the uncertainty is bigger than the industry

Growing from 58 TWh in 2014 to 176 TWh in 2023 is 13.1% a year. LBNL's low case for 2028 (325 TWh) is exactly the same 13.1%: it is not a slowdown scenario, it is the status quo. The high case doubles the pace to 26.9% a year. The gap between the two, 255 TWh, is larger than all US data centres used in 2023. Globally the IEA's 2035 range (700 to 1,700 TWh) spans 1,000 TWh, 2.4 times total data centre use in 2024. As a cross-check, the IEA's US share (about 45% of 415 TWh, about 187 TWh in 2024) sits right on LBNL's line. Plans built on a single forecast number are planning on a guess; plans that hold options across the fan are not.

So whatThe low case is the status quo, not a slowdown. Plan for the fan, not a single number.

How I derived this: Annual growth = (end / start)^(1 / years) minus 1: (176 / 58)^(1/9) = 13.1%; (325 / 176)^(1/5) = 13.1%; (580 / 176)^(1/5) = 26.9%. The 2018 point (about 76 TWh) is derived from LBNL's stated growth of about 7% a year from 2014 to 2018. The 2024 IEA cross-check multiplies the IEA's rounded US share by its global estimate.

What changes for infrastructure leaders

An AI roadmap with a GPU line and no megawatt line is not a plan yet.

Power is now a first-class capacity input, and the honest forecast is a range. Three things follow: energy per inference belongs next to latency and cost per token on the dashboard; capacity plans should name which case they assume and what triggers a switch; and efficiency work (distillation, batching, utilization) is a growth lever, because every TWh saved is a TWh you do not have to win in an interconnection queue.

How these numbers were produced

Global figures are transcribed from the executive summary of the IEA's Energy and AI report (April 2025). US figures are from LBNL's 2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report as summarised by the US Department of Energy (December 20, 2024): 58 TWh (2014), 176 TWh and 4.4% (2023), 325 to 580 TWh and 6.7% to 12% (2028), with stated growth of about 7% a year from 2014 to 2018. Everything else on this page (implied US totals, share of growth, growth rates, the 2018 point and the IEA cross-check) is my arithmetic on those published numbers; each insight shows the formula.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Rounded figures. The IEA describes these values as approximate ("around", "about"). I chart them exactly as stated: 415, 945, 700 and 1,700 TWh.
  • Mixed scenarios on one axis. The 2030 and 2035 columns are projections from different IEA cases, shown side by side for scale. They are not a forecast path and should not be connected into a trend line.
  • "AI" framing. The IEA figures cover all data centres, not only AI workloads. I use AI as the headline because the IEA identifies AI as the main driver of growth; the chart itself does not isolate AI.
  • Source access. iea.org blocked automated download when I checked, so figures were verified against the IEA executive summary text rather than a downloadable dataset. Please check the report before quoting.
  • Leadership implications are my opinion. The section on what changes for infrastructure leaders is my interpretation, not an IEA recommendation.
  • Implied US totals rest on rounded shares. 4.4%, 6.7% and 12% are rounded, so the implied US totals (4,000, 4,851 and 4,833 TWh) carry a few percent of error. The share-of-growth range (17.5% to 48.5%) should be read as roughly a sixth to a half.
  • Low with low, high with high. I pair LBNL's low data centre case with the total implied by 6.7%, and the high case with 12%, as LBNL presents them. Mixing cases would give a different ratio.
  • The 2018 point is derived. About 76 TWh is 58 TWh grown at LBNL's stated 7% a year for four years. It shows the shape of the acceleration and is not a reported value.
  • Two sources, two scopes. LBNL covers US data centres; the IEA covers the world and uses its own methods. The 2024 cross-check (about 187 TWh) only shows the two are consistent in size, not identical.
  • The trend line is not a forecast. The dotted line simply continues 2014 to 2023 growth to show that it lands on LBNL's low case.
