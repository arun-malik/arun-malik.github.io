How much agent capability survives a higher reliability bar

Arun Malik·December 9, 2026·Data story
Data StoryAI

A longer task horizon is not a dependable delegation budget. Requiring 80% rather than 50% predicted success removes 81.1% of the median evaluated configuration's horizon in METR Time Horizon 1.1. The threshold also reverses Claude Opus 4.6 and Gemini 3.1 Pro on point estimates, not statistically established superiority.

Agent capability is moving fast. But the leadership question is not just how long a task an agent can finish. It is how much task difficulty survives the success rate you require. A benchmark horizon at 50% predicted success is a useful capability signal. It is a poor substitute for a delegation acceptance criterion.

METR fits success probability against the time a human expert would take on controlled software, machine learning and cybersecurity tasks. Its 50% and 80% horizons are human-equivalent task difficulty, not agent runtime. Claude Opus 4.6's point estimate drops from roughly 12 human-equivalent hours at 50% success to about 70 minutes at 80%. Gemini 3.1 Pro drops from about 6.4 hours to about 90 minutes. The proof chart keeps the published uncertainty visible.

This is an archived benchmark snapshot accessed on November 28, 2026. METR says the page is no longer actively updated, a clarification dated September 8, 2026. It is not a complete survey of models available today. The YAML also includes three TH1.0 entries, which I exclude rather than silently mix benchmark versions.

What the raw data does not show at first glance

The chart above is the proof. The findings below are derived: they come from joining, re-cutting or reasoning across the data, and each one shows how it was derived.

Insight 1

The scope tax is model-specific, not a universal haircut

Across all 23 entries explicitly tagged TH1.1, the median scope reduction is 81.1%: the median retained horizon is just 18.9%. The reductions range from 75.0% for o3 to 90.3% for Claude Opus 4.6. Opus 4.6 falls from 718.8 human-equivalent minutes to 69.9, retaining 9.7%. Gemini 3.1 Pro falls from 384.1 to 89.8 minutes, retaining 23.4%. Gemini retains about 2.4 times the fraction of its 50% horizon that Opus does. This is a reduction in the fitted task-duration threshold, not a percentage of tasks, tokens or paid working hours lost.

The chart covers every TH1.1 configuration in the file. The card shows the six longest 50% horizons. Mythos Preview (early), marked with an asterisk, has a 50% estimate above METR's 16-hour reliability warning. These ratios are point estimates, not uncertainty-adjusted budgets.

So whatAsk for the scope at your required success rate, not a fixed discount on the headline horizon.

How I derived this:

How I derived this: filter results in the benchmark YAML to benchmark_name == METR-Horizon-v1.1. For each configuration, take H50 = metrics.p50_horizon_length.estimate and H80 = metrics.p80_horizon_length.estimate. Retained scope (%) = 100 * H80 / H50; scope reduction (%) = 100 * (1 - H80 / H50). Sort all 23 unrounded reductions and take the middle value for the median. Example: Opus = 100 * (1 - 69.874587 / 718.806830) = 90.2791%. Relative retention = (89.801503 / 384.147435) / (69.874587 / 718.806830) = 2.4048. Bars are ordered by descending H50, not by scope reduction.

Insight 2

A different success threshold produces a different shortlist

11 of 23 configurations change rank when the ranking moves from H50 to H80. Claude Opus 4.6 is second at 50% and third at 80%; Gemini 3.1 Pro moves from third to second. Opus leads Gemini by 87.1% at 50%, but Gemini leads Opus by 28.5% at 80%. Gemini 3 Pro moves from eighth to sixth, ahead of GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.5 at the higher bar. This is not a contradiction: the fitted success curves have different shapes.

Do not turn that reversal into a claim of proven superiority. Opus 4.6's published 80% interval is 27.0 to 170.4 minutes; Gemini 3.1 Pro's is 52.0 to 158.6. At 50%, their intervals are 316.7 to 3,633.8 and 233.5 to 694.8 minutes. They overlap at both thresholds. A rank from point estimates is an ordering of estimates, not a significance test.

So whatSet the acceptance threshold before choosing the model, and treat close point-estimate rankings as hypotheses to test.

How I derived this:

How I derived this: use the same 23 TH1.1 rows. Rank H50 and H80 separately in descending order, with rank 1 highest. Count rows where rank50 != rank80. No point estimates tie. Opus's H50 lead = 100 * (718.806830 / 384.147435 - 1) = 87.1%. Gemini's H80 lead = 100 * (89.801503 / 69.874587 - 1) = 28.5%. Read intervals directly from the corresponding ci_low and ci_high fields; do not compute rank confidence or a probability of superiority from marginal intervals.

Insight 3

Even inside the 80% horizon, individual tasks can fail every observed run

Joining the horizons to task outcomes makes the limitation concrete. Among the 158 Opus 4.6 tasks at or below its 69.9-minute H80, 146 succeeded on every observed run, nine had mixed outcomes, and three failed every run. Among the 161 Gemini 3.1 Pro tasks at or below its 89.8-minute H80, 153 succeeded on every run, seven had mixed outcomes, and one failed every run.

Both agents failed blackbox/beach on all six runs despite its 10-minute human duration estimate. Opus also failed all six runs of local_research/which_does_worse, a 4.091-minute task. The fitted horizon describes performance across a task distribution. It is not a minimum success rate promised for every task below that duration. These cohorts use different cutoffs, so they are not a head-to-head model test.

So whatValidate the actual task family you want to delegate; a short task is not automatically a safe task.

How I derived this:

How I derived this: map benchmark ID claude_opus_4_6_inspect to task agent Claude Opus 4.6 (Inspect), and gemini_3_1_pro to Gemini 3.1 Pro. Confirm the YAMLs share long_tasks_version and swaa_version. For each agent, keep tasks with 0 < human_minutes <= H80 and n_runs > 0, using the unrounded H80. Count each task once: all-success if n_successes == n_runs, all-failure if n_successes == 0, mixed otherwise. Share (%) = 100 * category_count / eligible_task_count. This is an unweighted task distribution, not METR's weighted fitted success rate. All-failure describes the observed attempts only.

What this means for engineering leaders

Choose the reliability bar before you choose the agent.

My recommendation: write the acceptance contract first. Define the task family, acceptable failure rate, verification evidence and escalation path. Then evaluate the model plus its tools and scaffold on representative work. A leaderboard measured at the wrong threshold answers the wrong procurement question.

A longer benchmark horizon is not a production delegation budget.

Even 80% predicted success is not an operational service-level guarantee. Use bounded assignments, independent checks and clear human handoffs where failures matter. Do not multiply the horizon into a staffing forecast or assume that splitting a task into smaller pieces makes the whole workflow reliable. These data do not measure production incidents, review costs, retries or end-to-end business outcomes.

How these numbers were produced

I downloaded METR's benchmark and task YAML files from their public asset URLs on November 28, 2026 and retained the raw snapshots under this story's data folder. The analysis uses all 23 result rows explicitly tagged METR-Horizon-v1.1, including the early Mythos Preview configuration. The proof chart shows the seven longest H50 point estimates with both thresholds and published confidence intervals; its table contains all 23. Derived insights cover per-configuration horizon ratios, rankings at each threshold and observed task outcomes below two configurations' fitted H80. The accompanying data/derive.py reproduces the chart tables and story metrics from the snapshots. It does not refit METR's curves. The methodology page supplies interpretation and limitations, not new benchmark measurements.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Snapshot, not today's frontier. Access date is November 28, 2026, not a model evaluation or dataset publication date. METR's page says it is no longer actively updated. This selection is not a comprehensive current-model leaderboard.
  • Version filter and unit of comparison. The asset includes 23 TH1.1 configurations and three TH1.0 configurations (GPT-2, davinci_002 and GPT-3.5 Turbo Instruct). Only TH1.1 is included. A configuration combines model, scaffold and evaluation setup; differences cannot be attributed to the base model alone. Human-readable labels map to IDs retained in the tables.
  • Human difficulty, not runtime. Horizons and task durations are in human-equivalent minutes. Most baselines use geometric means of successful human attempts; some durations use expert or QA estimates. Low-context contractors are not equivalent to employees familiar with their own codebase.
  • Controlled and narrow tasks. METR's tasks are self-contained, well-specified and automatically scored, primarily in software engineering, machine learning and cybersecurity. Messier work and holistic quality criteria can produce different outcomes. This is not a production reliability guarantee.
  • Horizon ratio is a derived proxy. Retained scope is H80/H50 and reduction is 1 minus that ratio. These are ratios of fitted duration thresholds, not shares of tasks automated, agent time, token cost, productivity or headcount.
  • Median weights configurations equally. The median uses all 23 unrounded per-configuration reductions, not a ratio of median horizons or a weighted average. Older configurations count as much as recent ones. The range rounds to 75.0% to 90.3%; the minimum unrounded reduction is 74.9595%.
  • Point estimates and uncertainty. Ratios, rankings, rank changes and relative leads use point estimates. Published bootstrap ci_low and ci_high values are shown directly in the proof and table. The YAML does not provide joint bootstrap draws for deriving calibrated ratio or rank intervals; none are invented. Overlap is not a formal test of equality or superiority.
  • Measurement ceiling. METR warns that horizons above 16 hours are unreliable with the current task suite. Mythos Preview (early) has an H50 of 17.4 hours and is flagged with an asterisk. Opus 4.6's H50 upper confidence bound also extends far beyond 16 hours. Such values are not validated long-duration capacities.
  • Chart selection and scales. The proof displays the seven largest H50 estimates and uses a logarithmic minute axis with full published intervals, without clipping. Its table and both ratio and rank analyses retain all 23. The card shows the six largest H50 estimates and the full-page ratio chart shows all 23. The 16-hour marker is a caution threshold, not a measured result.
  • Task join and denominators. Both YAMLs share suite version identifiers. The third insight selects each agent's tasks by its own unrounded H80, so cohorts differ. Tasks count equally and are not weighted by task_weight or n_runs. These distributions do not reproduce METR's weighted logistic fit.
  • Finite observed runs. All-success and all-failure describe recorded runs, not true probabilities of 100% or 0%. Six failures on one task are not proof it can never succeed. A fitted distribution-level 80% horizon does not establish 80% success on each individual task.
  • Rounding. Charts and narrative show minutes and percentages to one decimal, relative retention to one decimal and ranks as integers. Computation uses the full benchmark estimates, which are stored to six decimals. CSV-ready chart tables retain those published inputs. Approximate hours divide minutes by 60.
  • Leadership recommendations are judgment. Acceptance contracts, bounded assignments, independent verification and escalation are my engineering recommendations, not experimentally tested interventions in these datasets. No inference about staffing, production incidents or workflow reliability is made.
