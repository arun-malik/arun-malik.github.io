Developers' next AI wave is security and ops, exactly where trust is lowest

Arun Malik·December 2, 2026·Data story
Data StoryAI

Stack Overflow's 2026 survey shows what developers use AI for today. Re-cut the full 19-task table by momentum and a different story appears: the next wave is security and production operations, and only 6.6% of developers trust AI with important decisions.

The adoption debate is over. In Stack Overflow's 2026 Developer Survey, 83% of respondents use at least one AI tool at work, 77% of users use it daily, and 31% of daily users spend four or more hours a day with it. The chart above is the headline view: code in familiar areas, debugging and simple questions lead.

That ranking tells you where AI already is. It does not tell you where it is going. The survey also asks, for 19 tasks, whether developers expect to use AI more or less next year, and whether they refuse to use it at all. I re-cut that full table.

What the raw data does not show at first glance

The chart above is the proof. The findings below are derived: they come from joining, re-cutting or reasoning across the data, and each one shows how it was derived.

Insight 1

Security is the only task with below-median use and top-tier momentum

Plot all 19 tasks by how many developers use AI for them today against net momentum (expect to use more minus expect to use less). Most tasks sit on a diagonal: the more they are used, the more momentum they have. Finding or fixing security issues breaks the pattern. Only 34.6% use AI for it today, below the 47.4% median, yet 37.9% expect to use it more, the highest of every task in the survey, ahead of debugging (36.1%) and code generation (31.2%). Its net momentum of 31.3 points is second only to data and log analysis (31.8). Production operations sits at the far left with the highest refusal of any technical task: 36.0% do not use AI there and do not plan to.

So whatThe fastest-growing AI use case is not code generation. It is security.

How I derived this: Net momentum = "expect to use more" minus "expect to use less", in percentage points, per task. Medians are across all 19 tasks (use 47.4%, momentum 22.8). Bubble size is the share answering "do not use AI for this and do not plan to". All inputs are the published percentages for the workflow question (n=12,547); the metric and its ranking are mine.

Insight 2

Today's favourite uses are saturating; five tasks have more planned growth than current use

Divide "expect to use more" by "currently use" and call it the pull ratio. For generating code in a familiar area, the most popular use (69.3%), the ratio is 0.45, the lowest of all 19. Across tasks the correlation between current use and pull ratio is r = -0.92: the more a task is already covered, the less room is left. Five tasks have a ratio of 1 or more, meaning more developers expect to increase use than use AI for them today: production ops (1.17), security issues (1.10), multimedia (1.07), dev and test environments (1.02) and tickets and plans (1.01). Four of the five are operational or infrastructure work, not code generation.

So whatCode generation is saturating. The growth is moving into operations and infrastructure work.

How I derived this: Pull ratio = "expect to use AI more" / "currently use AI" for each task, using the published percentages (n=12,547). The two answers come from the same single-select grid per task, so a respondent who expects more is not necessarily a current user. r is the Pearson correlation across the 19 tasks between current use and pull ratio. It describes the published table, not causation.

Insight 3

Trust, not appetite, is the gate: only 6.6% trust AI with important decisions

Security and production ops are high-stakes work, and this is where trust runs out. Only 6.6% of developers trust AI output for many tasks including important decisions. The largest group, 48.0%, trust it only when they can easily verify the output. Developers already behave that way: just 9.6% use AI answers as-is, while 76.5% run them locally, 63.9% compare against the codebase and 53.0% inspect tests or security implications. Experience does not close the gap at the top: early-career developers are less favourable (53% vs 69% at 16+ years) and only 4% of them trust AI with important decisions. The next wave of adoption is heading into exactly the work that is hardest to verify.

So whatAdoption is heading straight into the work that is hardest to verify. Verification, not model quality, becomes the bottleneck.

How I derived this: Trust shares are the single-select trust question (n=14,304). Verification behaviour is a separate multi-select question (n=13,162), so it can sum past 100%. The early-career figures (1 to 5 years of experience) are quoted from Stack Overflow's summary text; I did not have the cross-tab table. Linking the trust gap to the security and ops momentum is my interpretation.

What this means for engineering and security leaders

Your developers are about to bring AI into security fixes and production operations whether or not you plan for it.

The bottleneck will not be model quality or licences; it will be verification. Invest ahead of the wave: reproducible environments, tests and policy checks that run on every AI change, runbooks that agents can follow and humans can audit, and review tooling that shows evidence rather than diffs. Make high-stakes work checkable, and trust will follow.

How these numbers were produced

All figures are from the published results tables of the AI section of the 2026 survey (full data view): the workflow question (19 tasks, n=12,547), the trust question (n=14,304), the verification question (n=13,162) and usage questions. Net momentum, pull ratio, medians and the correlation are my arithmetic on those percentages; each insight shows the formula. The 2025 survey is used for context only.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Self-selected sample. The survey is opt-in and skews toward Stack Overflow's audience. It describes respondents, not all developers.
  • Different bases per question. Each figure uses the respondents who answered that question (for example n=12,547 for current use, n=10,926 for hours per day among daily users). The stats on the card are not from one shared denominator.
  • Year-over-year comparisons are directional. 2025 and 2026 questions and samples differ, so I avoid computing changes between them.
  • Release timing. I cite the survey as the 2026 Developer Survey and the date I accessed it; I did not confirm the exact publication date.
  • Top five in the proof chart only. The proof chart shows the five most-used tasks for readability. The insights use all 19.
  • Shares, not people. "Expect to use more" and "currently use" are shares of the same respondents but not nested sets, so a pull ratio above 1 means more people expect growth than use AI today, not that every current user expects growth.
  • "Not part of my workflow" stays in the base. The published percentages include respondents for whom a task is not relevant. Production ops (22.0%) and security (19.1%) have many such respondents; excluding them would raise both use and momentum for those tasks.
  • Trust gap as a gate is my interpretation. The survey reports momentum and trust in separate questions. Linking them is my reading, not a finding stated by Stack Overflow.
  • Early-career figures are quoted. The 53% vs 69% favourability and 4% important-decision trust for 1 to 5 years of experience come from Stack Overflow's summary text, not a table I recomputed.
