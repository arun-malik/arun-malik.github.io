Most big cloud outages start with a change, not a storm

Arun Malik·December 14, 2026·Data story
Data StoryCloud reliabilitySRE

I classified every post-incident review Azure has published since late 2021. 45 of 79 were triggered by a deployment, a configuration change, a rotation or a vendor update. Since 2024 that share is 73%, and changes account for two thirds of the incidents that hit more than one region.

Three outages made November 2026 feel fragile. Outlook and Teams went down across more than 70 countries from November 12, and Microsoft pointed to settings applied to servers after an update. On November 13 a newly installed router flooded the FAA's telecom network with connection attempts and grounded US flights for four and a half hours. On November 16 Chase customers were locked out of online banking by what the bank called a software bug, not a cyberattack.

Two of those three trace back to something that changed. Is that a coincidence, or the normal shape of a large outage? News coverage cannot answer that, so I went to a dataset that can.

Microsoft publishes a post-incident review (PIR) for every Azure incident with broad customer impact. Each one has a timeline, a What went wrong section and the repairs. I downloaded all 79 reviews available on November 28, 2026, covering November 2021 to September 2026, and read the root cause of every one. Each review got one trigger: our own change, a vendor update, a physical failure (power, cooling, fiber, hardware) or no change cited (latent defects, load, attacks).

Most big cloud outages are not attacks or storms. They start with a change someone shipped.

45 of the 79 reviews, or 57%, start with a change: a code deployment, a configuration push, a certificate or key rotation, an automation run, a maintenance step or a vendor update such as the July 2024 CrowdStrike release. Physical failures account for 17. The other 17 cite no recent change at all.

What the raw data does not show at first glance

The chart above is the proof. The findings below are derived: they come from joining, re-cutting or reasoning across the data, and each one shows how it was derived.

Insight 1

Since 2024, nearly three in four reviewed incidents start with a change

From 2021 to 2023, 18 of 42 reviews (42.9%) were change-triggered. From 2024 to September 2026 it is 27 of 37 (73.0%). Every year from 2024 is at 67% or above, while no earlier year passes 50%. Physical failures fell from 10 reviews to 7, and reviews with no change cited fell from 14 to 3.

So whatAs platforms harden against hardware and load, the change pipeline becomes the main way production breaks. Fund rollout safety like you fund redundancy.

How I derived this:

How I derived this: group reviews by calendar year of the review date, then by two periods (2021 to 2023 and 2024 to 2026). Change share = (own change + vendor update) / reviews in the group. Per-year counts are in the chart table.

Insight 2

Changes spread wider than the other triggers

27 reviews describe impact in two or more regions or globally. 18 of them (66.7%) started with a change, against 51.9% of single-region incidents. A configuration pushed to a global edge network or a shared identity service lands everywhere at once. A failed transformer or a cut fiber stays local.

So whatGlobal config and shared control planes need the slowest, most staged rollout you run, not the fastest.

How I derived this:

How I derived this: in each review's What happened summary, count distinct Azure region names and flag global, worldwide or multiple-region wording. Multi-region = flag set or two or more regions. Then compute the trigger mix in each group.

Insight 3

Physical failures are rarer but last the longest

The median physical incident ran 11.8 hours from first impact to mitigation, against 8.5 hours for change-triggered ones and 5.2 hours where no change is cited. The longest review in the set is a 105-hour power and cooling event. Because changes are so common, they still add up to 53.2% of all impact hours.

So whatRollback fixes the common failure. Only another region fixes the long one. Plan and rehearse both.

How I derived this:

How I derived this: impact hours = mitigation time minus first-impact time from each review (78 of 79 have a window). Box plots show min, first quartile, median, third quartile and max per trigger group, computed with Python's statistics.quantiles.

What this means for your own platform. Treat every change as a potential incident. That means staged rollouts with automatic halts, a fast rollback that does not depend on the thing you just changed, and config and rotation pipelines held to the same safety bar as code. The physical side needs a different answer: capacity in another region, rehearsed, because those failures last longest.

This is one provider's published reviews, not a census of every cloud outage, and the trigger labels are my reading of Microsoft's own words. The method, every assumption and the full 79-row table are below so you can check or re-cut it.

How these numbers were produced

I froze the Azure status history PIR feed as data/azure-pirs-2026-11-28.json: 79 unique tracking IDs, each with title, date, full review text and the impact start and end times I parsed from the text. data/classification.csv holds one trigger label and a short note per review, assigned after reading the What went wrong or Root Cause section. data/derive.py recomputes every number and chart from those two files with Python's standard library and asserts the headline figures.

Durations run from first customer impact to mitigation as stated in each review. Where a review gives two windows, I used the span. Multi-region status comes from the What happened summary: two or more Azure region names, or the words global, worldwide or multiple regions.

Assumptions and limitations

  • One provider, published reviews only. Microsoft writes PIRs for incidents with broad impact. Smaller incidents, and incidents at other clouds, are not in this dataset. The results describe Azure's published major incidents, not all outages.
  • Trigger means the first event, not the deepest cause. Many reviews describe a change that exposed a latent bug. I label those as change, because without the change the incident would not have started that day. A reader who labels them as defects would get a lower change share.
  • Own change is broad. It includes code deployments, configuration pushes, certificate and key rotations, automation runs, operator actions and maintenance steps. Customer-initiated configuration sequences that triggered a platform bug (one review) are also counted as change.
  • Vendor update covers two reviews: the July 2024 CrowdStrike sensor update and an Ubuntu update. The CrowdStrike review states no single impact window, so it is excluded from duration charts.
  • No change cited groups latent defects, load and retry storms, attacks (two DDoS reviews) and one review with no stated cause. It means the review names no recent change, not that none occurred.
  • Durations are review windows. First impact to mitigation as Microsoft reports it. Customers saw different impact depending on region, service and failover setup. 78 of 79 reviews give a usable window.
  • Multi-region is a text match. Counting region names in the impact summary misses incidents described by service rather than region, and can count a region mentioned only as context. Treat 27 as approximate.
  • Period comparison has small samples. 2021 has 5 reviews (the feed starts in November 2021) and 2026 runs only to September. Reporting practice also changed over time, so the rise from 43% to 73% may partly reflect what Microsoft chose to review.
  • November 2026 news hooks are context. The Microsoft 365, FAA and Chase outages are described from news and reference sources cited below. They are not in the dataset and are not counted in any figure.
