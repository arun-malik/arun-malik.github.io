Most outages start with a change, not a storm

Arun Malik·December 14, 2026·Data story
Data StoryCloud reliabilitySRE

I classified 287 public outage write-ups from four independent sources: 182 company postmortems collected across the industry, plus the published incident reviews of AWS, Azure and Google Cloud. Three outages made November 2026 feel fragile. On November 13 a newly installed router flooded the FAA's telecom network with connection attempts and grounded US flights for four and a half hours. On November 16 Chase customers were locked out of online banking by what the bank called a software bug, not a cyberattack. And from November 12, Outlook and Teams went down across more than 70 countries after settings were applied to servers following an update.

Two of those three trace back to something that changed. Is that a coincidence, or the normal shape of an outage? News coverage cannot answer that, and neither can any one company's records. So I pooled four public sources that publish their root causes.

Across the industry: danluu/post-mortems, a long-running community list of public postmortems from companies such as GitHub, Cloudflare, Stripe, Slack, Reddit and CircleCI. I read all 240 entries and classified 182. The other 58 are 40 exclusions (security breaches, non-IT systems such as rockets and power grids, and entries too thin to label) and 18 AWS summaries that I count once, under AWS.

Across the clouds: every AWS Post-Event Summary (18, from 2011 to 2025), every Azure post-incident review in the public feed (79, from 2021 to 2026) and every Google Cloud status incident with a published analysis in the public feed (8, all from 2026). Each source is charted separately, so no single provider drives the result.

Each incident got one trigger, the first event in the write-up: a change (deployment, configuration or policy push, upgrade, migration, maintenance, operator action, vendor update), a physical failure (power, cooling, fire, fiber, hardware) or no change cited (latent bugs, load, attacks, a provider or dependency failing).

Most outages are not attacks or storms. They start with a change someone shipped.

Pooled, 163 of 287 (57%) started with a change. That is not one company's habit. Change is the most common trigger in every one of the four sources: 58% of company postmortems, 57% of Azure reviews, 63% of Google Cloud incidents and 44% of AWS summaries.

An independent survey points the same way. Uptime Institute's 2025 outage analysis reports that IT and networking issues caused 23% of impactful outages in 2024, driven mainly by change management and misconfiguration, and that 85% of human-error outages came from staff not following procedures or from flawed procedures. A survey and a set of write-ups measure different things, but they land in the same place: the riskiest moment is when someone changes production.

What the raw data does not show at first glance

The chart above is the proof. The findings below are derived: they come from joining, re-cutting or reasoning across the data, and each one shows how it was derived.

Insight 1

Change is the most common trigger in all four sources

Company postmortems: 105 of 182 (57.7%). Azure: 45 of 79 (57.0%). Google Cloud: 5 of 8 (62.5%). AWS: 8 of 18 (44.4%). AWS is lowest because a third of its summaries are power and weather events from 2011 to 2019, and even there change beats each of the other two triggers. Four independent publishers, writing for different audiences, land within 20 points of each other.

So whatNo single company's culture explains this. If you change production, a change is your most likely outage.

How I derived this: Group each dataset's classified incidents by trigger. Share = count / classified incidents in that dataset. The combined bar pools all 287, counting the 18 AWS summaries that also appear in the curated list only once.

Insight 2

Physical failures concentrate at the clouds that own the buildings

Power, cooling, fire, fiber and hardware cause 8.2% of company postmortems (15 of 182) but 21.5% to 33.3% of each cloud provider's reviews (23.8% pooled, 25 of 105). Companies that rent capacity still feel those events, as someone else's outage: 8 of the 62 company incidents with no change cited were a provider or upstream dependency failing.

So whatRenting the building moves physical risk to your provider. It does not remove it; it arrives as an incident you did not cause and cannot roll back.

How I derived this: Physical share = physical incidents / classified incidents per dataset; the pooled cloud figure combines the AWS, Azure and Google Cloud counts. Provider outages were counted from the classification notes of the cross-industry no-change group (notes that name a provider or upstream failure).

What this means for your own platform

Treat every change as a potential incident. The deploy pipeline is now the main way production breaks.

That means staged rollouts with automatic halts, a fast rollback that does not depend on the thing you just changed, and config, certificate and key rotation pipelines held to the same safety bar as code. Global configuration and shared control planes deserve the slowest rollout you run, because that is where a change spreads widest.

The physical side needs a different answer. If you run on a cloud, most of it happens in your provider's buildings and arrives as their outage, and no rollback of yours fixes it. Only capacity in another zone or region does, and only if you have rehearsed the failover.

These are published write-ups, not a census of every outage. Teams choose what to publish, and the trigger labels are my reading of their own words. The method, every assumption and the full table of 287 incidents plus the 40 exclusions are below so you can check or re-cut it.

How these numbers were produced

I froze four public datasets on November 28, 2026: the danluu/post-mortems list at commit 630562e (company, section and link per entry in data/danluu-postmortems-2026-11-28.json), the 18 AWS Post-Event Summaries (data/aws-pes.csv), the 79 Azure post-incident reviews (data/azure-pirs-2026-11-28.json) and the Google Cloud public incident feed (data/google-cloud-incidents-2026-11-28.json). Trigger labels and a one-line note per incident are in data/danluu-classification.csv, data/aws-pes.csv, data/classification.csv and data/google-cloud-classification.csv. data/derive.py recomputes every number and chart from those files with Python's standard library and asserts the headline figures.

For the provider sources I read the root cause section of each review. For the curated list I labelled each entry from the list's own summary of the write-up. The list does not carry dates for most entries, so it is used for shares only, not trends.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Published write-ups only. Teams choose which incidents to write up, and a curated list favours notable, technical stories. The shares describe public postmortems and provider reviews, not every outage. Nobody can measure all outages from public data.
  • Trigger means the first event, not the deepest cause. Many write-ups describe a change that exposed a latent bug. I label those as change, because without the change the incident would not have started that day. A reader who labels them as defects would get a lower change share.
  • Change is broad. It covers code deployments, configuration and policy pushes, certificate and key rotations, upgrades, migrations, automated scaling, maintenance steps, operator commands and vendor updates such as the July 2024 CrowdStrike release.
  • No change cited groups latent defects, load and retry storms, attacks, expiring certificates and outages at a provider or dependency. It means the write-up names no recent change, not that none occurred.
  • 40 curated entries are excluded: 16 security breaches that were not service outages, 12 non-IT or chip-design failures (rockets, spacecraft, power grids, a ship, a medical device, a CPU), 9 entries too thin to classify, and 3 that were not service outages (a project failure, a UI bug and a desktop client bug). They are listed in the table as Excluded.
  • Duplicates are counted once. All 18 AWS Post-Event Summaries also appear in the curated list; they are counted only under AWS, with the same label. Older Google, Microsoft and other cloud write-ups in the curated list that are not in a provider feed stay in the cross-industry group.
  • Small provider samples. AWS has published 18 summaries in 14 years, and Google Cloud's public feed holds 8 incidents, all from 2026. Their shares are shown for comparison and should not be read as precise rates.
  • Uptime Institute is a survey, not a dataset I combined. Its figures come from operator surveys and its own incident database, and are quoted as an independent cross-check only.
  • November 2026 news hooks are context. The FAA, Chase and Microsoft 365 outages are described from news and reference sources cited below. They are not in the dataset and are not counted in any figure.
